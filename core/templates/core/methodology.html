Methodology

Everything on your dashboard comes from your reading export plus a couple of free book databases. Here's how each bit is worked out. Tap a section to open it.

{# ── Comparative Analytics: intro (open by default) ── #}

How Comparative Analytics Work

The Comparative Analytics card on your dashboard shows three stats, and compares each one against a global average and your community percentile. The two are quite different things:

  • Community percentiles are real data. If the card says you read longer books than 70% of readers, that's worked out from everyone who's uploaded to Bibliotype, and it'll shift as more people join.
  • Global averages are rough estimates. Books per year comes from the big reading surveys, so that one's fairly solid. Nobody really measures the other two, so they're educated guesses (there's more on each below). Your community percentile is the more useful number. Once enough people have joined, I'd like to swap these for figures from Bibliotype's own readers.
{# ── Book Length ── #}

Average Book Length

What it measures

The average page count of the books on your read shelf, rounded to the nearest page.

How we work it out

We take page counts from your export and fill in the gaps from Open Library and Google Books. Books we can't find a page count for are left out.

Community percentile

The percentage of readers whose average book is shorter than yours.

Worth knowing

  • While we're still fetching book details, some page counts will be missing. The number updates once that's done.
  • Editions of the same book can vary a lot in length. We use whichever edition Open Library or Google Books gives us first, which might not be the one you read.
  • Nothing gets trimmed, so one giant omnibus edition can bump your average up quite a bit.

{# ── Book Age ── #}

Average Book Age

What it measures

The average year the books on your read shelf were first published. Basically, do you read new releases or older stuff?

How we work it out

We take publication years from your export and fill in the gaps from Open Library and Google Books. Books we can't find a year for are left out.

Community percentile

Your average year is ranked against every other reader's. A higher percentile means you read newer books than most people here.

Worth knowing

  • While we're still fetching book details, some years will be missing. The number updates once that's done.
  • We try to use the original publication year rather than the year of a reprint, but sometimes the databases only have an edition date, so we use that.
  • A few very old classics will drag your average back a long way.

{# ── Books Per Year ── #}

Books Per Year

What it measures

How many books you finish in a typical year.

How we work it out

We count the books with a finish date and divide by the number of years in which you finished at least one. So 50 books spread across 5 years works out at 10 a year.

Community percentile

The percentage of readers who get through fewer books a year than you.

Worth knowing

  • Books without a finish date don't count, so if you often mark books as read without a date, your pace will look lower than it really is.
  • Years where you didn't finish anything are skipped, so taking a few years off reading won't drag your average down.
  • If all your dated books fall in the same year, we skip the percentile and just show your total.

{# ── Fetching genres ── #}

How We Fetch Genres

What it does

Goodreads exports don't include genres at all, and StoryGraph only gives a few tags, so we look every book up ourselves.

How it works

We search two free, public book databases: Open Library first, then Google Books. Open Library has "subjects" for each book (things like space opera or grief) and Google Books has categories. If your StoryGraph export has tags we use those too. We find each book by its ISBN if there is one, or by title and author if not.

Worth knowing

  • Sometimes we grab the wrong book, or can't find it at all, especially for obscure editions or books without an ISBN.
  • This happens in the background, so genres can keep filling in for a minute or two after your dashboard loads.
  • The labels are whatever these databases decided, and they don't always agree with each other.
{# ── Flattening & classifying genres ── #}

How We Simplify & Classify Genres

The problem

The labels we get back are a mess. "Fiction, Fantasy, General", "epic fantasy" and "sword & sorcery" all mean roughly the same thing, and one book can come back with a dozen of them.

How it works

We have a big list of aliases that maps every label onto one of about 56 genres (fantasy, history, romance, memoir and so on). Then we keep the five most specific genres for each book, or six if the fifth is something like fantasy or thriller, so you see "space opera" instead of just "Fiction".

Fiction vs non-fiction

Some labels, like "classic", "young adult" and "children's", could go either way. We only count those as non-fiction if the book also has something like history or biography. What the databases say wins over shelf names people have typed.

Worth knowing

  • It's all rules, so it gets things wrong sometimes. Poetry goes under fiction, for example, just to keep things simple.
  • If it's not clear whether a book is fiction or non-fiction, we leave it where it is.
  • People argue about genres all the time, so you might not agree with every label.
{# ── Mainstream score ── #}

Mainstream Score

What it measures

The percentage of your books by a big-name author or from one of the big publishers (Penguin Random House, Hachette, HarperCollins, Macmillan and Simon & Schuster) and their imprints.

How it works

We check whether each book's author or publisher is on our mainstream list, then divide by your total number of books. A high score means you mostly read well-known names, and a low one means you read more indie and lesser-known stuff.

Worth knowing

  • It's not a judgement either way, there's nothing wrong with reading popular books!
  • A book with no known publisher only counts as mainstream if its author does.
  • The big publishers own loads of smaller imprints, so we won't catch every one.
{# ── Most positive & negative review ── #}

Most Positive & Negative Review

What it does

If you write reviews on Goodreads or StoryGraph, we find your most glowing one and your harshest one.

How it works

We go through every review you've written that's more than a few words long and score it with VADER, an open-source sentiment analysis tool that rates how positive or negative a bit of text sounds. For the positive one we pick the warmest of your 5-star reviews, and for the negative one the harshest of your 1-star reviews. If you don't have any 5 or 1-star reviews, we just pick from everything you've written.

Worth knowing

  • It's easily fooled by sarcasm, mixed feelings or quotes.
  • Really short reviews are skipped because there isn't enough to go on.
  • If you don't write reviews, this bit just won't show up on your dashboard.
{# ── Recommendations ── #}

How We Recommend Books

This is the most complicated one. The basic idea is to find readers with similar taste to yours, then suggest books they loved that you haven't read yet.

1. How similar are two readers?

We compare two libraries in seven different ways and combine them into one similarity score. Two of them use some well-known maths:

  • Cosine similarity compares the mix of genres and authors you read with someone else's, without caring about how much either of you reads.
  • Jaccard similarity is how many books you've both read, out of all the books either of you has read.

The other five look at how you both rated the same books, your shared favourites, whether you rate harshly or generously, and which decades you tend to read from.

2. Collect books to suggest

We take the books your most similar readers (plus some anonymised libraries) rated highly, and throw out anything you've already read.

3. Rank them

Books score higher if more of your similar readers loved them, if they're rated well overall, if they fit your favourite genres, and if they're like what you're reading right now.

4. Mix it up

Lastly we limit how many books can come from one author or series, so you don't get your whole list from the same trilogy.

Worth knowing

  • They get better as more people upload, and work best if you've logged a decent number of books yourself.
  • You're only matched with readers who've left recommendation visibility on.
  • Every suggestion is a real book that someone similar to you rated highly. No AI makes them up.
