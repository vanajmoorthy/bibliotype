Methodology

Everything on your dashboard is built from your reading export plus a couple of free book databases. Here's how each part is worked out — tap any section to open it.

{# ── Comparative Analytics: intro (open by default) ── #}

How Comparative Analytics Work

The Comparative Analytics card on your dashboard shows three stats, and for each one it gives you two things to compare against: a global average and your community percentile. This section covers what each stat measures, how it's built from your data, and how much to trust each number.

One distinction matters more than anything else here, so we want to be upfront about it.

  • Community percentiles are real data. They come straight from every Bibliotype library. When the card says you read longer books than 70% of readers, that number is measured from actual uploaded shelves, and it shifts as more people join.
  • Global averages are rough estimates. The books-per-year figure is a median pulled from the big reading surveys, so it's on firm ground. The other two are rougher working estimates, since nobody actually measures them well. They're honest reference points rather than precise truths, and we've shown our working for each one below. Lean on your community percentile; treat the global average as a backdrop. Once enough readers have joined, we'd like to derive all of these from the community's own libraries instead.
{# ── Book Length ── #}

Average Book Length

What it measures

The mean page count of every book on your read shelf that has one, rounded to the nearest whole page.

How your value is computed

Bibliotype pulls each book's page count from your export and fills the gaps from Open Library and Google Books. Only books with a resolved page count make it into the average. Anything still missing one sits it out.

Community percentile

Your average is ranked against the same figure for every other reader. The percentile is the share of readers whose average book length is shorter than yours.

Assumptions and caveats

  • If enrichment is still in progress, some books may not yet have a page count and the figure will update automatically once enrichment completes.
  • Different editions of the same book can have very different page counts. Bibliotype uses whichever edition Open Library or Google Books returns first, which may not match the edition you read.
  • Very short books (e.g. novellas) and very long ones (e.g. omnibus editions) are included without trimming, so a single unusually long book can shift your average noticeably.

{# ── Book Age ── #}

Average Book Age

What it measures

The mean original publication year of all books on your read shelf that have a known publish year, giving a sense of whether you tend to read newer releases or reach further back into the backlist.

How your value is computed

Bibliotype pulls the publication year from your export and fills the gaps from Open Library and Google Books. Only books with a resolved publication year count toward the average.

Community percentile

Your average publish year is ranked against the same figure for every other Bibliotype reader. A higher percentile means your reading skews more recent compared to the community; a lower percentile means you tend to read older books.

Assumptions and caveats

  • If enrichment is still in progress, some books may be missing a publish year and the figure will update automatically once enrichment completes.
  • Bibliotype uses the original publication year where possible, not the year of a reprint or new edition. If Open Library or Google Books only provides an edition date, that is used instead.
  • Books from ancient or medieval periods can pull the average down significantly if you read a lot of classics.

{# ── Books Per Year ── #}

Books Per Year

What it measures

Your average annual reading pace, calculated only from books with a recorded finish date in your export.

How your value is computed

Bibliotype counts the number of books with a recorded finish date, then divides by the number of distinct calendar years in which you finished at least one book. For example, if you finished 50 books spread across 5 different years, your pace is 10 books a year.

Community percentile

Your pace is ranked against every other reader's. The percentile is the share of readers who get through fewer books a year than you do.

Assumptions and caveats

  • Only books with a recorded finish date count. If you mark books as read on Goodreads without entering a date, they are excluded and your pace may appear lower than reality.
  • The calculation uses the number of years in which you finished at least one book, not the total span between your first and last recorded finish date. A reading gap of several years does not pull your average down.
  • If you finished only one book or all your books fall in the same calendar year, the community percentile comparison is omitted and a simpler total-books count is shown instead.

{# ── Fetching genres ── #}

How We Fetch Genres

What it does

Your export barely comes with genres — Goodreads gives none at all, and StoryGraph only gives a few tags — so for every book we go and look them up.

How it works

We search two free, public book databases: Open Library first, then Google Books. Open Library gives us a book's "subjects" (things like space opera, British fiction, or grief); Google Books gives us its categories. If your StoryGraph export already has tags, we use those too. We match a book by its ISBN where we have one, and otherwise by title and author.

Assumptions and caveats

  • Matching isn't perfect. An obscure edition, an unusual title, or a book missing an ISBN can match the wrong record or nothing at all.
  • Enrichment happens in the background, so genres can keep filling in for a minute or two after your dashboard first appears.
  • The labels reflect how these databases and their communities catalogued a book, not a single official truth — two databases can disagree.
{# ── Flattening & classifying genres ── #}

How We Simplify & Classify Genres

The problem

Raw genre labels are messy and inconsistent. "Fiction, Fantasy, General", "epic fantasy", and "sword & sorcery" all mean roughly the same thing, and a single book can arrive tagged a dozen different ways.

How it works

We map every raw label onto one of about 56 tidy canonical genres — fantasy, history, romance, memoir, and so on — using a large dictionary of aliases. Then we keep the five most specific genres per book (six if the fifth is a very specific fiction genre like fantasy or thriller), so a book is described by what makes it distinctive rather than a generic "Fiction".

Fiction vs non-fiction

Some labels are genuinely ambiguous — "classic", "young adult", and "children's" can be either. We only flip those to non-fiction when the book also carries a clear non-fiction marker such as history or biography. A clear signal from the databases always beats a shelf name you (or another reader) typed.

Assumptions and caveats

  • It's rule-based, so it isn't flawless. Poetry, for instance, we file under fiction as a deliberate simplification.
  • A book only gets reclassified when the evidence is clear — we'd rather leave an ambiguous book where it is than flip it on a weak signal.
  • Genre is inherently fuzzy. Two thoughtful readers will disagree about where a book belongs, and so will two databases.
{# ── Mainstream score ── #}

Mainstream Score

What it measures

The share of your books that come from a big-name author or one of the major publishing houses and their many imprints — Penguin Random House, Hachette, HarperCollins, Macmillan, and Simon & Schuster.

How it works

For every book we check whether its author or its publisher is flagged as mainstream, then divide by your total number of books. A high score means you mostly read widely-published, well-known names; a low score means you reach for more independent or under-the-radar writers.

Assumptions and caveats

  • "Mainstream" is a rough flag, not a value judgement. Niche isn't better or worse than popular — it's just a different way to read.
  • A book with no known publisher only counts as mainstream if its author does.
  • Publishing imprints are tangled in the real world (big houses own dozens of smaller labels), so matching a book to a major publisher is approximate.
{# ── Most positive & negative review ── #}

Most Positive & Negative Review

What it does

If you write reviews in Goodreads or StoryGraph, we surface your most glowing one and your most scathing one.

How it works

We look at every review you wrote that's more than a few words long and score its tone with VADER, a well-known open-source sentiment analysis tool — it reads a piece of text and rates how positive or negative it sounds. For the positive pick we prefer your 5-star reviews and choose the warmest-toned one; for the negative pick we prefer your 1-star reviews and choose the harshest. If you have no 5- or 1-star reviews, we fall back to the most positive and most negative tone across everything you wrote.

Assumptions and caveats

  • Tone-scoring is blunt. Sarcasm, mixed feelings, and quoting someone else can all fool it.
  • Very short reviews are skipped, since there isn't enough text to read a tone from.
  • If you don't write reviews, this part of your dashboard simply doesn't appear.
{# ── Recommendations ── #}

How We Recommend Books

This is the most involved one, so bear with us. The core idea is simple: find readers whose taste looks like yours, then suggest the books they loved that you haven't read yet.

Step 1 — measure how similar two readers are

We compare any two libraries across seven angles and blend them into a single similarity score. Two of those angles use standard maths worth naming:

  • Cosine similarity — measures how closely two lists of preferences point in the same direction, regardless of how much each person has read. We use it to compare your genre mix and your favourite authors against someone else's.
  • Jaccard similarity — the share of books two readers have in common out of all the distinct books they've read between them. It rewards genuinely overlapping shelves.

The other five angles look at how you both rated the same books, your shared top books, how harsh or generous your ratings tend to be, and which decades you read from.

Step 2 — gather candidates

From the readers most similar to you (plus a sample of anonymised community libraries), we collect the books they rated highly and strip out anything you've already read or rated poorly.

Step 3 — score and rank

Each candidate earns points for how many similar readers loved it, how highly it's rated overall, how well it fits your favourite genres, and whether it matches what you're currently reading.

Step 4 — keep it varied

Finally we stop any one author or series from dominating, so you get a spread rather than ten books from the same trilogy.

Assumptions and caveats

  • Recommendations get better as more people upload their libraries, and they need a reasonable reading history of your own to work well.
  • Anonymous uploads can only be matched against readers who have chosen to share their data.
  • We only ever suggest real books that other readers actually rated highly — nothing is invented from thin air.
